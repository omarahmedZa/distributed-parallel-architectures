Module 3 – Advanced Parallel Processing and Optimization

Lecture 9 — Performance Modeling, Scalability and Optimization

Model and evaluate parallel performance using strong/weak scaling, Amdahl’s and Gustafson’s laws, communication-to-computation analysis, and profiling, and identify scalability limitations and optimization strategies.

Why this matters

Scalable performance is not automatic: it depends on serial fractions, overheads, communication, and resource balance. Modeling and measurement reveal where time goes and which architectural or algorithmic changes matter most.

Prerequisites: Fundamentals — speedup & efficiency, Lecture 5, Lecture 8.

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish strong and weak scaling and interpret scaling results.
  • Apply Amdahl’s and Gustafson’s laws to reason about speedup limits and scaled workloads.
  • Use communication-to-computation ratio and overhead concepts to diagnose bottlenecks.
  • Explain the role of performance profiling in optimization and identify common scalability limitations.

Core ideas and mechanisms

Strong vs weak scaling

  • Strong scaling: fixed total problem size, increasing p. Goal: reduce time. Limited by serial fractions and growing overhead/communication per unit work.
  • Weak scaling: problem size grows proportionally with p (fixed work per processor). Goal: maintain constant time. Stresses memory, communication, and global operations as scale grows.

Amdahl’s law (fixed workload)

If a fraction f of a program is serial (not parallelizable) and 1-f is perfectly parallelizable, then:

S(p) ≤ 1 / ( f + (1-f)/p ) ,   lim(p→∞) S(p) = 1/f

Even small serial fractions cap maximum speedup in strong scaling.

Gustafson’s law (scaled workload)

For workloads scaled with p, let the serial time be s and parallel time per processor be q (normalized so s+q=1 on the parallel system). The scaled speedup relative to the time a single processor would need for the larger problem is:

S_scaled(p) = s + p·q = p - s(p-1)

Scaling problem size can maintain efficiency and realize larger useful speedups when parallel work grows.

Communication-to-computation ratio

Let T_comp be computation time and T_comm communication/synchronization time (including overheads). Efficiency suffers when T_comm / T_comp is large. Increasing p often reduces T_comp per process but may not reduce, or may increase, T_comm (more messages, collectives, contention), harming strong scaling.

Performance profiling and optimization

Profiling (time, hardware counters, tracing, communication analysis) identifies hotspots: compute-bound kernels, memory stalls, load imbalance, synchronization waits, or network contention. Optimization strategies include improving locality, reducing/overlapping communication, balancing load, algorithmic changes (communication-avoiding methods), and architecture-aware tuning.

Worked examples

Example 1 — Amdahl’s law

Let serial fraction f=0.10 (90% parallel). For p=8:

S(8)=1/(0.10 + 0.90/8)=1/(0.10 + 0.1125)=1/0.2125≈4.71×, efficiency ≈58.8%.

For p→∞, S_max=1/0.10=10×. Doubling processors yields diminishing returns as overhead/serial work dominates.

Example 2 — Gustafson’s scaled speedup

On p=8 processors a scaled run spends 5% time in serial work and 95% in parallel work (s=0.05, q=0.95):

S_scaled(8)=0.05 + 8×0.95=0.05 + 7.6=7.65× (scaled speedup), indicating effective use of processors when problem size grows with resources.

Example 3 — Communication-to-computation ratio

Per iteration: T_comp=40 ms, T_comm=10 ms on 16 processes (ratio 0.25). Scaling to 64 processes reduces compute to ~10 ms but collective/communication costs rise to 12 ms (ratio 1.2), so total time increases or barely improves—an efficiency collapse visible in profiling/traces.

Architectural design decisions and trade-offs

  • Optimize for strong or weak scaling: depends on application goals (time-to-solution vs larger problems/throughput).
  • Reduce serial bottlenecks: algorithm redesign, parallel I/O, asynchronous coordination.
  • Balance system resources: compute vs memory bandwidth vs network (machine balance) to avoid shifting bottlenecks.
  • Overlap and locality: hide latency (Lecture 5) and improve data reuse (Lectures 2, 7).

Limitations and scalability

  • Non-parallelizable work, synchronization, and contention impose hard or practical limits.
  • Strong scaling runs out of work per processor; weak scaling stresses global communication and memory capacity.
  • Profiling overhead and measurement perturbation can obscure fine-grained effects.

Research problems

  • Performance modeling for heterogeneous, hierarchical, and exascale systems.
  • Automated performance diagnosis and autotuning across architectures.
  • Communication-avoiding/synchronization-reducing algorithms and energy-aware optimization.

Recap and check for understanding

  1. What does Amdahl’s law say about a workload with 5% serial fraction?
    Show answer

    Maximum speedup is bounded by 1/0.05=20×, even with infinitely many processors, under strong-scaling assumptions.

  2. How can weak scaling show good speedup when strong scaling does not?
    Show answer

    By increasing problem size with processor count, the parallel portion can grow enough to keep processors utilized (Gustafson’s perspective), even if fixed-size speedup is limited by serial fractions/overheads.

Research Activities

Literature review — Paper 1

Citation G. M. Amdahl, “Validity of the Single Processor Approach to Achieving Large Scale Computing Capabilities,” in AFIPS Conf. Proc. (Spring Joint Computer Conference), vol. 30, 1967, pp. 483–485. DOI: 10.1145/1465482.1465560.

Core contribution: Articulates the fundamental limitation that the serial portion of a workload bounds the achievable speedup from parallel execution.

Method/architecture: Analytical reasoning about fractions of sequential versus parallelizable computation in large-scale computing systems.

Key results: Establishes the speedup bound later formalized as Amdahl’s law, shaping expectations for strong scaling and parallel system design.

Limitations: Assumes a fixed workload and idealized parallelization; does not account for scaling problem sizes (addressed by Gustafson) or detailed overhead/architecture effects.

Literature review — Paper 2

Citation J. L. Gustafson, “Reevaluating Amdahl’s Law,” Communications of the ACM, vol. 31, no. 5, pp. 532–533, May 1988. DOI: 10.1145/42411.42415.

Core contribution: Argues that in practice problem sizes often scale with available parallel resources, leading to scaled speedup that can grow nearly linearly.

Method/architecture: Presents an alternative speedup formulation based on fixed execution time with expanded parallel workload, supported by empirical observations from parallel applications.

Key results: Shows that large parallel systems can be effectively utilized when workloads scale, complementing Amdahl’s fixed-size perspective.

Limitations: Assumes the parallel portion can grow meaningfully and that overheads/memory/communication do not dominate; not all applications admit proportional scaling.

Case study — TOP500 supercomputer scaling (Frontier, ORNL)

What it is: Frontier is an HPE Cray EX supercomputer at Oak Ridge National Laboratory that achieved exascale performance on the HPL benchmark, ranking highly on the TOP500 list.

Architectural design: A massively parallel, heterogeneous system combining CPUs and GPUs with high-bandwidth memory and a high-performance interconnect, designed to balance compute, memory, and communication for large-scale workloads.

How it addresses lecture challenges: Demonstrates weak-scaling/capability performance through extreme parallelism, fast interconnects, and optimized software stacks, while highlighting the importance of communication, memory, and energy efficiency at scale.

Observed strengths/limitations: Exceptional peak and benchmark performance for suitably scalable workloads; real application scaling varies with serial fractions, communication-to-computation ratios, I/O, and load balance—illustrating practical limits beyond theoretical models (system details per public ORNL/TOP500 descriptions).
