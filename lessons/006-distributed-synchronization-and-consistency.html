Module 2 – Distributed Systems Architecture

Lecture 6 — Distributed Synchronization and Consistency

Understand distributed synchronization challenges—clock synchronization, mutual exclusion, and coordination—and compare consistency models and their architectural trade-offs.

Why this matters

Distributed systems lack a single global clock and shared memory, yet must order events, share resources safely, and present meaningful views of replicated state. Synchronization and consistency choices define correctness, performance, and availability.

Prerequisites: Lecture 4, Lecture 2 (consistency intuition), Fundamentals.

Learning objectives

  • Explain why physical clocks drift and how logical/physical synchronization is approached.
  • Describe distributed mutual exclusion and coordination mechanisms.
  • Compare consistency models (strong to eventual/causal intuitions) and their trade-offs.
  • Relate synchronization/consistency decisions to latency, availability, and scalability.

Core ideas and mechanisms

Clock synchronization

Each node has a local clock; crystals drift and network delays vary. Physical clock synchronization (e.g. NTP-style protocols, Cristian’s algorithm in concept) aims to bound skew relative to real time but cannot make clocks perfectly identical. Logical clocks (Lamport clocks, vector clocks) capture causal ordering (“happened-before”) without relying on precise physical time: if event A causally precedes B, logical timestamps reflect that order (with different strengths/limitations).

Distributed mutual exclusion

Mutual exclusion ensures only one process enters a critical section at a time. Distributed approaches include:

  • Centralized coordinator: simple but a bottleneck/single point of failure.
  • Token-based: possession of a token grants access; avoids some contention but token loss/recovery is complex.
  • Permission/quorum-based: processes request and obtain permission from a set (e.g. Ricart–Agrawala, Maekawa-style quorums) trading message complexity, fault tolerance, and latency.

Distributed coordination

Coordination includes leader election, consensus/agreement, barriers, and group membership. In asynchronous systems with failures, fundamental limits (e.g. impossibility of guaranteed consensus with one faulty process in fully asynchronous settings, as shown by FLP) motivate practical systems to use timeouts, partial synchrony assumptions, or randomized/replicated protocols (e.g. Paxos/Raft families in practice) to achieve coordination.

Consistency models

Model (intuition)Guarantee sketchTypical trade-off
Strong/linearizableOperations appear instantaneous in a single global order consistent with real timeSimpler reasoning; higher latency/coordination, reduced availability under partitions
SequentialSome global order consistent with each process’s program orderStrong but not tied to real-time; still coordination costs
CausalCausally related operations seen in order; concurrent ops may differPreserves causality with better performance/availability than strong models
EventualReplicas converge if updates stop; may read stale values temporarilyHigh availability/low latency; applications handle anomalies/conflicts

Consistency choices interact with replication and network partitions; systems often balance consistency, availability, and latency based on application requirements.

Architectural design decisions and trade-offs

  • Ordering guarantees vs performance: stronger ordering/consistency increases coordination and latency.
  • Centralized vs distributed control: simplicity vs fault tolerance/scalability.
  • Quorum size/replication: affects availability, latency, and consistency strength.
  • Failure assumptions: timeouts and synchrony assumptions shape liveness and safety in practice.

Limitations and scalability

  • Clock skew and variable network delay complicate time-based reasoning.
  • Coordination protocols incur message overhead and may block under failures/partitions.
  • Strong consistency can limit global scalability and availability; weak models shift complexity to applications.

Research problems

  • Low-latency coordination and consensus at geo-distributed scale.
  • Consistency models and interfaces that match application semantics efficiently.
  • Fault-tolerant synchronization under adversarial/Byzantine or heterogeneous conditions.

Recap and check for understanding

  1. Why are logical clocks useful in distributed systems?
    Show answer

    They capture causal ordering of events without requiring perfectly synchronized physical clocks, enabling reasoning about happened-before relationships.

  2. What is a key trade-off of stronger consistency models?
    Show answer

    They simplify correctness reasoning but typically require more coordination, increasing latency and potentially reducing availability/scalability under failures or partitions.

Research Activities

Literature review — Paper 1

Citation L. Lamport, “Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System,” Communications of the ACM, vol. 21, no. 7, pp. 558–565, Jul. 1978. DOI: 10.1145/359545.359563.

Core contribution: Defines the happened-before relation and introduces logical clocks to order events in distributed systems without relying on synchronized physical clocks.

Method/architecture: Formalizes causal ordering and presents algorithms for logical timestamps and total ordering using logical time.

Key results: Establishes foundational principles for reasoning about event ordering and synchronization in distributed computations.

Limitations: Logical clocks capture causality but not real-time ordering; vector clocks (and related mechanisms) are needed to fully characterize concurrency/causal relationships in some settings.

Literature review — Paper 2

Citation G. Ricart and A. K. Agrawala, “An Optimal Algorithm for Mutual Exclusion in Computer Networks,” Communications of the ACM, vol. 24, no. 1, pp. 9–17, Jan. 1981. DOI: 10.1145/358527.358537.

Core contribution: Proposes a distributed mutual exclusion algorithm based on message exchanges and logical ordering to grant critical-section access without a central coordinator.

Method/architecture: Nodes broadcast requests and defer/reply according to timestamped requests, ensuring safety and ordering properties under stated assumptions.

Key results: Achieves mutual exclusion with a clear message-based protocol and analyzes message complexity and correctness.

Limitations: Requires reliable communication and participation; message overhead grows with the number of nodes, and fault tolerance/failures require additional mechanisms.

Case study — Google Spanner (globally distributed database)

What it is: Google Spanner is a globally distributed, synchronously replicated database that provides externally consistent (linearizable) transactions at global scale.

Architectural design: Uses sharding/replication across datacenters with Paxos-based replication groups and a time API (TrueTime) that exposes bounded clock uncertainty to order transactions consistently.

How it addresses lecture challenges: Combines coordination/consensus with tightly bounded time uncertainty to achieve strong consistency and synchronization guarantees across wide-area networks.

Observed strengths/limitations: Enables strong semantics for globally distributed applications; trade-offs include higher commit latency due to coordination and dependence on specialized time infrastructure and careful engineering to bound uncertainty.
