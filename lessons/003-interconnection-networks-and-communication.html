Module 1 – Advanced Parallel Computer Architectures

Lecture 3 — Interconnection Networks and Communication Architectures

Analyze network topologies (Mesh, Torus, Fat-Tree, Hypercube), switching techniques, and routing algorithms, and evaluate latency/bandwidth trade-offs in parallel and distributed systems.

Why this matters

Cores, caches, memory controllers, and nodes can only cooperate as fast as the interconnect allows. Topology, switching, and routing choices directly shape latency, bandwidth, contention, cost, and scalability.

Prerequisites: Fundamentals (parallel/distributed models), Lecture 1 (scaling/organization), Lecture 2 (coherence/NUMA traffic).

Learning objectives

  • Describe and compare Mesh, Torus, Fat-Tree, and Hypercube topologies.
  • Explain circuit, packet, and wormhole switching and their performance implications.
  • Distinguish routing algorithm goals (minimality, adaptivity, deadlock/livelock freedom).
  • Evaluate latency, bandwidth, bisection bandwidth, diameter, and cost/scalability trade-offs.

Core ideas and mechanisms

Network topologies

A topology defines how nodes/switches are connected. Key properties include degree (links per node), diameter (max shortest-path hops), bisection bandwidth (bandwidth across the minimum cut splitting the network), and cost/regularity.

TopologyStructureStrengthsLimitations
Mesh (2D/3D)Grid of nodes; neighbors connected along dimensionsSimple, regular, good locality mapping, scalable wiringLarger diameter; edge/center traffic imbalance
TorusMesh with wraparound linksLower diameter than mesh, symmetry, better bisectionLonger wraparound wires/cost; routing complexity
Fat-TreeMulti-level tree with higher bandwidth toward rootHigh bisection bandwidth, good for all-to-all/collectivesSwitch/cabling cost; upper-level contention possible
HypercubeNodes connected if addresses differ in one bit (log N degree)Low diameter (log N), rich connectivityDegree grows with size; wiring/packaging complexity

Switching techniques

  • Circuit switching: reserves an end-to-end path before transfer. Good for long, steady flows; setup overhead and poor utilization for bursty traffic.
  • Store-and-forward packet switching: each hop buffers the whole packet before forwarding. Simple but per-hop latency grows with packet size.
  • Wormhole switching: forwards flits (small flow-control units) in a pipeline; header sets route and body follows, reducing latency and buffer needs but making links/blocking sensitive to contention (can cause blocking across the path).
  • Virtual cut-through: forwards before full packet receipt when output is free, reducing latency versus store-and-forward.

Routing algorithms

Routing selects paths for messages. Desirable properties: correctness, deadlock freedom, livelock freedom, and good load balance.

  • Deterministic (e.g. dimension-order/XY): same path for a source-destination pair; simple and deadlock-free with proper design, but can concentrate traffic.
  • Oblivious: path independent of current load (may randomize), balancing load statistically.
  • Adaptive: reacts to congestion/faults; improves performance under imbalance but needs mechanisms to avoid deadlock/livelock.

Worked example: hop-count intuition

In an n×n 2D mesh, worst-case shortest path (corner to corner) is about 2(n-1) hops; a torus reduces this by wraparound links (roughly half in that worst case). For n=8, mesh diameter ≈ 14 hops vs torus ≈ 8 hops, illustrating lower average/worst-case latency at added wiring complexity. (Exact values depend on routing and link model.)

Limitations and scalability

  • Contention/hotspots reduce effective bandwidth below peak.
  • Deadlock, livelock, and fairness become critical at scale.
  • Cost, power, and cabling/packaging constrain degree and topology choices.
  • Mapping application communication patterns to topology strongly affects realized performance.

Research problems

  • High-radix, low-diameter, energy-efficient interconnects for exascale and data centers.
  • Congestion-aware/adaptive routing and quality-of-service under diverse traffic.
  • Reconfigurable/photonic interconnects and co-design with applications/runtimes.
  • Fault-tolerant routing and resilience to link/switch failures.

Recap and check for understanding

  1. Why can a fat-tree be attractive for collective-heavy workloads?
    Show answer

    It provides high bisection bandwidth, supporting many simultaneous communications across the network without a single narrow bottleneck (given sufficient switching capacity).

  2. What is a key risk of wormhole switching under congestion?
    Show answer

    A blocked packet can hold buffers/links across multiple hops, propagating contention and stalling other flows.

Research Activities

Literature review — Paper 1

Citation C. E. Leiserson, “Fat-Trees: Universal Networks for Hardware-Efficient Supercomputing,” IEEE Transactions on Computers, vol. C-34, no. 10, pp. 892–901, Oct. 1985. DOI: 10.1109/TC.1985.6312192.

Core contribution: Proposes fat-tree interconnection networks that provide high bandwidth near the root, enabling efficient communication for a broad class of parallel workloads.

Method/architecture: Analyzes a tree-structured network with increasing channel capacity toward higher levels and discusses routing and universality properties.

Key results: Shows fat-trees can be hardware-efficient and communication-efficient (near-universal) compared to fixed-bandwidth trees, supporting scalable supercomputing communication.

Limitations: Practical implementations must manage switch cost, cabling, and contention; real performance depends on routing, traffic patterns, and engineering constraints.

Literature review — Paper 2

Citation W. J. Dally and C. L. Seitz, “Deadlock-Free Message Routing in Multiprocessor Interconnection Networks,” IEEE Transactions on Computers, vol. C-36, no. 5, pp. 547–553, May 1987. DOI: 10.1109/TC.1987.1676939.

Core contribution: Develops theoretical conditions and practical methods for deadlock-free routing in multiprocessor interconnection networks.

Method/architecture: Uses channel dependency analysis and structured routing restrictions (e.g. virtual channels/turn models in later practice) to prevent cyclic wait conditions.

Key results: Provides foundational techniques ensuring deadlock freedom while enabling efficient routing in direct networks such as k-ary n-cubes/meshes.

Limitations: Restrictions to guarantee deadlock freedom can reduce path diversity or adaptivity; balancing deadlock avoidance with performance and fault tolerance remains an ongoing design challenge.

Case study — InfiniBand fat-tree clusters in HPC systems

What it is: Many production HPC clusters use InfiniBand switched fabrics, often deployed in fat-tree (or Clos-like) topologies to connect compute nodes with low latency and high bandwidth.

Architectural design: Multi-level switch hierarchy provides multiple paths and high bisection bandwidth; adaptive/oblivious routing and link technologies are engineered for MPI collectives and general cluster traffic.

How it addresses lecture challenges: Delivers scalable bandwidth and predictable performance for mixed communication patterns, mitigating bisection bottlenecks seen in simpler topologies at cluster scale.

Observed strengths/limitations: Strong throughput for communication-intensive HPC workloads; costs include switch/cable infrastructure and sensitivity to congestion, routing configuration, and oversubscription choices (deployment-specific, but the topology trade-offs are well established).
